Sharing is the norm

≈ 1.5 min

In species-rich meadows most plants are generalists and share most of their visitors. Sharing is a problem for a plant: pollen is lost to other species (consumption, wrong stigmas) and foreign pollen arrives on its own stigma (heterospecific pollen, HP). Central question of the whole talk: how do plants in such a meadow still get enough of their own pollen (conspecific pollen, CP)?

Why should timing matter?

≈ 1 min

  • Only a tiny fraction of pollen reaches a conspecific stigma
  • Pollen presentation theory: plants can dose pollen to pollinators (Harder and Thomson 1989)
  • With shared pollinators, when pollen is offered may decide who carries it

Optional: add Harder & Wilson 1994, LeBuhn & Holsinger 1998 and Stone et al. 1998 to techroom/references.bib (from Psani_clanku/2021/START_paper/pollen_presentation_references.bib).

Pollen is lost through consumption, grooming and transfer to the wrong species. Gradual dispensing should pay off with many, inefficient pollinators; dumping all pollen at once should pay off with few, efficient ones.

Pollen must flow

≈ 1 min

Štenc et al. (2025)

Pollinator performance = visitation activity per flowering stalk × conspecific pollen grains on the body. Succisa is served by hoverflies early in the morning (Eristalis tenax, Helophilus), Centaurea and Trifolium by Apis in the afternoon. The plants split the day: diurnal stratification of pollen transfer lowers the cost of sharing pollinators.

From body to stigma

≈ 1 min

Pollen on the body
Pollen on the stigma

Define terms once: conspecific pollen (CP) = own species; heterospecific pollen (HP) = other species. Single-visit deposition (SVD) = what one visit leaves. Terminal pollen deposition (TPD) = the final load on an old stigma. Focal species from now on: Succisa pratensis, a core species of the meadow network (Part 4).

The law of large numbers

≈ 1.5 min

Monte Carlo simulation parameterised by empirical SVD and visitation rates: accumulation of many independent, noisy visits is sufficient to reproduce the stable terminal CP loads observed in the field. HP is different: not predicted by the null model, depends on the floral neighbourhood, and is diluted by high conspecific floral density. Štenc et al., submitted to New Phytologist.

Within a locality, plants taller than their neighbours receive about 11 % more conspecific pollen per SD of height. Height does not affect HP. Larger flowerheads collect slightly more HP. But: traits explain almost none of the plant-level variance (ICC 0.793 → 0.794). Height shifts the mean; most plant-to-plant variation remains position, microhabitat or chance.

Pollen deposition through a flower’s life

≈ 2 min

Redraw the GAMM figure: the confidence bands are broken and “Unknown” / “SC” add noise. Show SI vs SIC only. Literature synthesis: 36 species, 23 families, 49 surveys, 194 time-stamped records.

Meta-analysis of published deposition time series (Štenc, in prep.). Mating system best predicts the curve shape (deviance explained 56.8 %). Self-incompatible species accumulate pollen over a broader part of flower life (median AUC 0.72 vs 0.14 in SIC; p_adj = 0.024) but rarely reach P/O ≥ 1 (1 of 7 surveys). Long accumulation without saturation: a sign of chronic pollen limitation in obligate outcrossers. Bridge back to Succisa: protandrous, long-lived stigmas, hence the value of many visits.

A stable core

≈ 1.5 min

k-core decomposition per year, null model of 1 000 permutations per year. Six species are central in the sharing network across 15 years: Centaurea jacea, Selinum carvifolia, Succisa pratensis, Pimpinella saxifraga, Achillea ptarmica, Ranunculus acris. Plant identity explains 38 % of centrality variance beyond floral abundance (44 %). Note that the two best-studied species of Parts 2 and 3, Succisa and Centaurea, are both core species.

Drought concentrates visits on the core

≈ 2 min

Recurring summer drought in 2011–2025. The core holds about 14 % of flowers but receives 67 % of visits on average, rising from 34–55 % (2011–14) to 84–88 % (2023–25). In dry years peripheral flowering drops about threefold, core flowering does not.

A more fragile network

≈ 1.5 min

Co-extinction simulation following Memmott, Waser, and Price (2004)

Losing the core costs more in dry years and more every year

Co-extinction simulations (Memmott et al. 2004). Core-first removal: R from 0.69 (2011) to 0.41–0.43 (2023–25). Robustness gap grows by about 0.022 per year. Link to pollen: if most visits go to a few core plants, those plants share almost every insect. What does that mean for the pollen arriving on their stigmas? That is the open question of Part 5.

The price of being in the core

≈ 1 min

Project “Apparent competition bottleneck” (pitch stage). Hypothesis: when drought crowds pollinators onto the core, core plants share nearly every insect. This should increase heterospecific pollen deposition on core stigmas and may be a hidden cost that prevents competitive exclusion.

Time of day as a niche axis

≈ 1 min

Project DiuHanPolNet (manuscript draft). Diurnal stratification structures the network: “global generalists, local specialists”.

  • Hourly interaction rewiring ≈ 34 %
  • Diurnal stratification reduces apparent competition by > 40 % per year
  • Pollinators shift foraging time away from dominant competitors (Apis, dominant Diptera)
