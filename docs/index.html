What is pollination?

≈ 1 min

Pollen is produced
in the anthers
›
An animal carries it
to another flower
›
It must land on a stigma
of the same species

→ fertilisation → seeds

For the non-specialists: plants cannot move, so to mate they need a courier. Pollen from one flower has to reach the stigma of another flower of the same species. Pollen landing on the wrong species is lost, and can even block the stigma. The three photos are from our meadow: Succisa anther, a hoverfly (Eristalis interruptus) covered in pollen, a Succisa stigma.

Ollerton et al. 2011: about 87.5 % of flowering plant species are animal-pollinated (higher in the tropics, about 78 % in temperate communities). Klein et al. 2007: 87 of 115 leading global food crops (75 %) benefit from animal pollination. Potts et al. 2016 (IPBES assessment): 5–8 % of global crop production is directly attributable to animal pollination, worth US$ 235–577 billion per year. Decline: e.g. Hallmann et al. 2017 found a more than 75 % drop in flying insect biomass over 27 years in German protected areas. Bridge: pollination is not one plant and one bee. In a meadow like ours, dozens of plants share the same insects. That is the starting point of our work.

Sharing is the norm

≈ 1.5 min

Not only bees: in our meadow hoverflies, flies, beetles, butterflies and moths all visit flowers (photos: burnet moth on Sanguisorba, Eristalis on Achillea, butterflies on Succisa). In species-rich meadows most plants are generalists and share most of their visitors. Sharing is a problem for a plant: pollen is lost to other species (consumption, wrong stigmas) and foreign pollen arrives on its own stigma (heterospecific pollen, HP). Central question of the whole talk: how do plants in such a meadow still get enough of their own pollen (conspecific pollen, CP)?

Why should timing matter?

≈ 1 min

  • Only a tiny fraction of pollen reaches a conspecific stigma
  • Pollen presentation theory: plants can dose pollen to pollinators (Harder and Thomson 1989)
  • With shared pollinators, when pollen is offered may decide who carries it

Optional: add Harder & Wilson 1994, LeBuhn & Holsinger 1998 and Stone et al. 1998 to techroom/references.bib (from Psani_clanku/2021/START_paper/pollen_presentation_references.bib).

Pollen is lost through consumption, grooming and transfer to the wrong species. Gradual dispensing should pay off with many, inefficient pollinators; dumping all pollen at once should pay off with few, efficient ones.

Preference and constancy

≈ 1 min

Interview stick: offer two flowers, record which one the insect chooses next

  • Preference: which flower is chosen more often than expected
  • Constancy: staying on the same species in consecutive visits, more than preference alone predicts

Two different behaviours that are often mixed up. Preference = choosing one flower type over another. Constancy = repeating the previous choice. A constant pollinator carries purer loads, so constancy matters directly for heterospecific pollen. Interview stick (Thomson 1981): a stick carrying two flowers is offered to a foraging insect; the choice relative to the flower it just left is recorded. Arrays of real or artificial flowers do the same with many flowers at once. The transition matrix shows how often an insect moves from each species to each other species; constancy is the excess on the diagonal.

How do pollinators choose?

≈ 1 min

3D-printed flowers, 3 % sucrose, Eristalis tenax · Matoušková, Štenc, and Janovský (2023); Haveldová et al. (submitted)

Bridge: the field tells us who visits. To see how an individual pollinator decides, we went to the lab. A: arena with two flower forms in a chessboard pattern, 15 cm apart, so the fly must fly between flowers. B: each flight is scored as constant (same form) or switching. C: four forms, yellow or white, large or small. D: in the field E. tenax visits mostly Succisa. E: in the fly’s colour space, the white form sits next to Succisa, Centaurea and Selinum, the yellow form next to Leontodon. So the artificial flowers mimic real core plants of the meadow. Matoušková et al. 2023 (Biol. J. Linn. Soc.): naive E. tenax select colour most strongly, then size, symmetry only weakly; yellow is preferred, especially when large.

Experience changes the choice

≈ 1 min

  • Naive flies (lab-reared): strong innate preference for yellow
  • Experienced flies (caught in the meadow): no colour preference left
  • Flower constancy 10–15 % lower after experience

5 270 visits by naive and 31 973 by experienced flies · Haveldová, Štenc, Matoušková & Janovský (submitted)

Share of all visits that went to yellow forms, summed over the four forms (naive: YL 1 829 + YS 1 682 of 4 841 visits; experienced: 8 000 + 7 855 of 31 973). Caveat: visits were not spread evenly across treatments, so this is a summary of the raw counts, not the model. The GLMs show significant colour, size and interaction effects in both groups (Table 1 of the manuscript). Experienced flies from a flower-rich meadow are more opportunistic: weaker preferences and lower constancy. They also reacted more to which flowers they could see from their position (angular area ratio). Implication for the plant: an experienced hoverfly arriving on Succisa has likely just visited something else.

Define terms once: conspecific pollen (CP) = own species; heterospecific pollen (HP) = other species. Single-visit deposition (SVD) = what one visit leaves. Define terms explicitly: - CP = Conspecific Pollen (own species) - HP = Heterospecific Pollen (foreign pollen from neighbours) - SVD = Single-Visit Deposition: measured experimentally with virgin bagged stigmas exposed to exactly 1 visit. - TPD = Terminal Pollen Deposition: the final load on naturally pollinated stigmas.

One visit is a lottery

≈ 1.5 min

One visit leaves only ≈ 2 % of the body’s own pollen on the stigma · no difference among Eristalis, Helophilus and Bombus

SVD experiments 2022–2024 on bagged Succisa inflorescences (124 single visits; 300 pollinators swabbed for body loads). Mean body load: CP 148 grains (SD 394), HP 479 (SD 1 102). Only about 2.3 % of body CP and 0.1 % of body HP end up on the stigma in one visit. Kruskal–Wallis among dominant morphogroups: CP H3 = 1.59, p = 0.66; HP H3 = 5.20, p = 0.16. Per visit, the pollinators are functionally equivalent, which fits the Freudenfeld result: what matters is how many visits, not who makes them.

But the final load is stable

≈ 1.5 min

CV of conspecific pollen: body 129 % → single visit 206 % → terminal load 69 %. Heterospecific pollen stays variable: body 264 % → terminal 147 %. Variance partitioning of TPD (693 stigmas, 273 plants, 2022–2024): years and days explain little CP variance; plants 15 %, stigmas 82 %. Within 2024, days were not significant (CP χ² = 1.32, p = 0.52). HP is different: year (χ² = 32.4, p < 0.001) and plant (χ² = 104.7, p < 0.001) both matter.

The law of large numbers

≈ 1.5 min

Monte Carlo simulation parameterised by empirical SVD and visitation rates: accumulation of many independent, noisy visits is sufficient to reproduce the stable terminal CP loads observed in the field. HP is different: not predicted by the null model, depends on the floral neighbourhood, and is diluted by high conspecific floral density. Štenc et al., submitted to New Phytologist.

Buffered in time and in space

Step 1: context. Height of 194 flowering stalks on 100 plants, median 32 cm; about 60 % are 25–40 cm, only 3 % above 50 cm. So most plants sit on the rising part of the next curve. Step 2: own pollen per stigma ~ quadratic height, negative binomial GLMM with plant/flower random effects: linear z = 4.2, p < 0.001; quadratic z = −3.6, p < 0.001; predicted peak at about 50 cm. Pollen deposition through a flower’s life

≈ 2 min

Redraw the GAMM figure: the confidence bands are broken and “Unknown” / “SC” add noise. Show SI vs SIC only. Literature synthesis: 36 species, 23 families, 49 surveys, 194 time-stamped records.

Meta-analysis of published deposition time series (Štenc, in prep.). Mating system best predicts the curve shape (deviance explained 56.8 %). Self-incompatible species accumulate pollen over a broader part of flower life (median AUC 0.72 vs 0.14 in SIC; p_adj = 0.024) but rarely reach P/O ≥ 1 (1 of 7 surveys). Long accumulation without saturation: a sign of chronic pollen limitation in obligate outcrossers. Bridge back to Succisa: protandrous, long-lived stigmas, hence the value of many visits.

What is the core?

≈ 1 min

Photo: Colin, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0
All plants

What remains = core

k-core decomposition (Seidman 1983), done separately for each year. Think of an onion: remove plants with few sharing links, repeat until only a mutually connected centre remains. A species counts as core if it is central more often than expected from its flower abundance (null model, 1 000 permutations per year).

A stable core

A whole-day network makes everyone look like a generalist. A morning visitor carries ≈ 50 % own pollen, an afternoon visitor ≈ 7 %

293 swabbed visitors of Succisa, Handrkov 2024 · confirms Štenc et al. (2025) with new data

This brings the community result back to pollen. Swabs (same method as before) from insects caught on Succisa through the day in 2024. Own pollen ~ hour: quasi-Poisson slope −0.33 per hour, p < 0.001. Foreign pollen ~ hour: slope 0.01, p = 0.88. Share of own pollen ~ hour: quasi-binomial slope −0.44 per hour, p < 0.001. Single visits to virgin stigmas (124 visits, 2022–2024) also left the most own pollen at 8:00 (≈ 9 grains vs ≈ 2 later), but the trend is only marginal (p = 0.07). Meaning: Succisa’s morning release is not a curiosity. Visits in the first hours move most of its own pollen; later visits mostly bring foreign pollen.
