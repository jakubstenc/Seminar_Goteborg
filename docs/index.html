Beyond flower-visitor lists

Everything I will present today is the product of an incredible team effort. Since 2011, our group around Zdeněk Janovský at Charles University has been running a permanent living lab at the Handrkov meadow. We don’t just record who visits a flower: we catch the insects, count pollen grains on their bodies under the microscope, track stigmas through time, and follow pollen from anthers all the way to seed set. This talk reflects a shared journey: 15 continuous seasons, over 45,000 interactions, and hundreds of thousands of counted pollen grains.

What is pollination?

For the non-specialists: plants cannot move, so to mate they need a courier. Pollen from one flower has to reach the stigma of another flower of the same species. Pollen landing on the wrong species is lost, and can even block the stigma. The three photos are from our meadow: Succisa anther, a hoverfly (Eristalis interruptus) covered in pollen, a Succisa stigma.

Not in a vacuum

≈ 1 min

Pollination does not happen as an isolated interaction between one flower and one dedicated visitor. In species-rich meadows, dozens of plants co-flower and share the same pollinator guild. Every visit is embedded in a complex web of direct and indirect interactions.

Plants can’t choose visitors, but they can choose when

Transition to Part 2: we move from meadow-wide networks down to the scale of the pollen grain. 1. The dilemma: anthers produce millions of grains, but < 1% ever reaches a conspecific stigma. Over 99% is eaten by hoverflies and bees, groomed away, or dropped on foreign stigmas. 2. Harder & Thomson 1989: because visits have diminishing returns, dumping all pollen at once causes massive wastage. Dosing pollen gradually across visits maximizes the number of carriers and recipient mates. 3. In a shared meadow, pollinators have distinct daily activity curves. When a plant releases its pollen determines which pollinator guild it attracts and how much foreign pollen it avoids.

But does it reflect pollen transfer?

≈ 0.5 min

But does visitation reflect actual pollen transfer?

We have seen that visitation matches diurnal pollen availability (START I, Štenc et al. 2023). But does a visit equal pollination? Observing visits only tells us who lands on flowers. It doesn’t tell us: 1. Whether pollen is actually picked up. 2. How many grains the insect carries (load). 3. How pure that pollen load is (conspecific vs heterospecific). To bridge the gap between flower visitation and actual pollen flow, we captured foraging insects and swabbed their bodies. This brings us to START II (Štenc et al. 2025, PPEES).

Community transport vs visits

≈ 1 min

Visits ≠ Pollen flow — flower visits drastically overestimate transport for Succisa & Sanguisorba, but double-underestimate Selinum & Potentilla.

Pollen loads reweighted to each group’s visit distribution · Handrkov 2020–2021 · Freudenfeld et al. (2025)

Here we compare each plant species’ share of community flower visits (grey) against its share of all carried pollen (rose).

  1. The discrepancies:
  • Centaurea and Ranunculus are balanced (they get ~26% and ~18% of visits and move ~29% and ~18% of pollen).
  • Selinum and Potentilla are massively over-represented: they get only 7% and 5% of visits, but account for 14% and 9% of all transported pollen (+2×).
  • Conversely, Succisa, Sanguisorba, and Trifolium are severely under-represented: Succisa gets 11% of visits but only 4% of transported pollen (3× less); Sanguisorba gets 8% visits but 3% pollen; Trifolium gets 3% visits but under 0.5% pollen (7× less).
  1. What does this mean?
  • Traditional plant-pollinator networks based solely on visits assume that every visit moves pollen equally. This assumption fails: visit networks overestimate transport for plants like Succisa, while drastically underestimating transport for plants like Selinum.
  • Visit frequency does not equal functional pollen transport.
  1. The effect of pollen production (Future testing):
  • A crucial open question is pollen production: Does an umbel of Selinum dominate the pollen pool simply because it produces orders of magnitude more pollen grains than a Succisa head?
  • In future work, we are directly measuring pollen production per flower/inflorescence across all species to disentangle the effect of sheer grain abundance from flower morphology and insect grooming.

Define terms once: conspecific pollen (CP) = own species; heterospecific pollen (HP) = other species. Single-visit deposition (SVD) = what one visit leaves. Defining the deposition steps

≈ 1 min

1. Pollinator Body Load
CP (Conspecific)
HP (Heterospecific)

Pollen carried by visitor.
Can be own species (CP) or foreign (HP).

2. Single Visit (SVD)

Pollen left by one visit on virgin stigma.
Mechanically inefficient & noisy (high CV).

3. Terminal Load (TPD)

Final cumulative load on old stigma.
Accumulated visits buffer noise (low CV).

Define terms explicitly: - CP = Conspecific Pollen (own species) - HP = Heterospecific Pollen (foreign pollen from neighbours) - SVD = Single-Visit Deposition: measured experimentally with virgin bagged stigmas exposed to exactly 1 visit. - TPD = Terminal Pollen Deposition: the final load on naturally pollinated stigmas.

One visit is a lottery

≈ 1.5 min

One visit leaves only ≈ 2 % of the body’s own pollen on the stigma · no difference among Eristalis, Helophilus and Bombus

SVD experiments 2022–2024 on bagged Succisa inflorescences (124 single visits; 300 pollinators swabbed for body loads). Mean body load: CP 148 grains (SD 394), HP 479 (SD 1 102). Only about 2.3 % of body CP and 0.1 % of body HP end up on the stigma in one visit. Kruskal–Wallis among dominant morphogroups: CP H3 = 1.59, p = 0.66; HP H3 = 5.20, p = 0.16. Per visit, the pollinators are functionally equivalent, which fits the Freudenfeld result: what matters is how many visits, not who makes them.

But the final load is stable

≈ 1.5 min

CV of conspecific pollen: body 129 % → single visit 206 % → terminal load 69 %. Heterospecific pollen stays variable: body 264 % → terminal 147 %. Variance partitioning of TPD (693 stigmas, 273 plants, 2022–2024): years and days explain little CP variance; plants 15 %, stigmas 82 %. Within 2024, days were not significant (CP χ² = 1.32, p = 0.52). HP is different: year (χ² = 32.4, p < 0.001) and plant (χ² = 104.7, p < 0.001) both matter.

The law of large numbers

≈ 1.5 min

Monte Carlo simulation parameterised by empirical SVD and visitation rates: accumulation of many independent, noisy visits is sufficient to reproduce the stable terminal CP loads observed in the field. HP is different: not predicted by the null model, depends on the floral neighbourhood, and is diluted by high conspecific floral density. Štenc et al., submitted to New Phytologist.

Buffered in time and in space

Who wins from splitting the day?

≈ 1 min

Reduction in apparent competition from diurnal stratification · 15-year mean (2011–2025) · DiuHanPolNet

Who gains the most from splitting the day? 1. In plants, the biggest winners are the peripheral species: Lathyrus pratensis (−45%), Trifolium pratense (−37%), Lotus corniculatus (−36%), and Potentilla erecta (−33%). These are the exact species that were dominated in space! By foraging at temporal fringes (morning and afternoon), they escape the midday bully rush and cut their apparent competition by more than a third. 2. The core drivers (Centaurea jacea, Succisa pratensis) gain the least (−8%). They don’t need to hide: they dominate the crowded midday hours. 3. In pollinators, subordinate foragers (Zygaena moths −45%, muscid flies −32%, small hoverflies −28%) escape dominant competitors (Apis, Bombus −12% to −13%) by using temporal refuges. Space and time act as orthogonal escape routes for biodiversity.

What actually governs each interaction: space, year, or time?

Up until now, networks have mostly been viewed as static webs. In reality, interactions vary across three fundamental physical dimensions: 1. Space: micro-spatial clumping within plots. 2. Year: macro-climate shocks and boom-and-bust population dynamics across 15 seasons. 3. Time: diurnal phenology and hourly schedules. The question is: does the entire network respond as a whole, or is each pairwise interaction governed by a different axis?

Outlook: Beyond static networks

≈ 1 min

Every link in the network is governed by a different axis of nature.

Dyadic variance partitioning of apparent competition (608 plant–plant links) · Handrkov 2011–2025 · Štenc et al. (in prep.)

Novel conceptual framework: Instead of asking what the network looks like as a static graph, we deconstruct what DRIVES each link. 1. Network modularity is not just a topological artifact—modules are governed by different physical dimensions! 2. Spatial aggregation governs the core (76% space): Dominant composites (Centaurea, Succisa) are bound by micro-spatial clumping within plots. 3. Climate volatility governs the generalist bridges (up to 44% year): White umbels (Heracleum, Daucus) link modules via boom-and-bust insect years. 4. Diurnal phenology buffers the subordinates (-45% PAC): Subordinate legumes (Lathyrus, Trifolium) escape spatial bullies via morning temporal refuges. Every edge in an ecological network has its own mechanistic driver profile.

Backup: Who drives whom?

≈ 1 min

The core feeds the pollinators that everyone else shares.

Müller’s index of potential for apparent competition (PAC), per year 2011–2025 · species present ≥ 10 years · point = mean, bar = interquartile range

PAC is directional. PAC(A → B) is high when the pollinators visiting B get most of their food from A, so A’s flowers “drive” B’s visitors. Net role = outgoing − incoming PAC. All six core species are net drivers (Centaurea +3.9, Succisa +2.4, A. ptarmica +2.4, R. acris +2.1, Pimpinella +2.0, Selinum +1.6). Core species are drivers in 91 % of species-years, peripheral species in 12 %. Only Sanguisorba comes close among the rest. Meaning: the peripheral plants are visited by pollinators that the core keeps alive. For pollen this means foreign pollen on peripheral stigmas will mostly be core pollen, and the core receives little foreign pollen in return.

Backup: Where a plant grows matters

≈ 1 min

Clumps attract visitors; dense neighbours take them away.

Fine-scale 1 m² mapping of flowers in 93 plots × 16 years, 86 plant species · HanSpaNet (in prep.)

After time, space. In every plot we map flowers at 1 m² resolution, so for each plant species we know how many flowers it has, how clumped they are, and how many and how mixed its neighbours are. Backup: Space shapes apparent competition

≈ 1 min

Identity sets the driver; dense clumps and flower numbers deliver the blow.

Dyadic mixed model across 96,364 co-occurring plant pairs in 93 plots × 15 years · PAC × HanSpaNet

Does space affect the potential for apparent competition (PAC), and how? We tested pairwise apparent competition (PAC from neighbour to focal plant) across 96,364 co-occurring pairs in 93 plots over 15 years, controlling for donor species, recipient species, and year random effects. 1. Neighbour identity is by far the biggest effect (+0.40 SD, p < 10⁻⁸): when a neighbour is one of the dominant “thieves” (Succisa or Centaurea), apparent competition on the focal plant surges. 2. Neighbour abundance (+0.08 SD, p < 10⁻¹²⁰): having more flowers than the focal plant consistently increases the pressure. 3. Spatial clumping (+0.033 SD, p < 10⁻²⁷): independent of flower abundance, a neighbour growing in dense clumps exerts significantly stronger apparent competition than an evenly dispersed neighbour. 4. Focal plant clumping (+0.028 SD, p < 10⁻¹⁹): dense focal clumps also draw in shared generalist foragers. So apparent competition is not just a whole-meadow property: it is physically structured by fine-scale floral clumps.

Backup: Who wins and who loses in space?

≈ 1 min

Core drivers dictate the visitors; the periphery pays the price.

Species-level driver power minus vulnerability from dyadic mixed models · 96,364 pairs across 93 plots × 15 years

When we map species along their net competitive role in space (driver power minus target vulnerability): 1. The clear WINNERS: Centaurea jacea, Succisa pratensis, Achillea ptarmica, Ranunculus acris, Selinum carvifolia. These are the core species. They have massive baseline attractiveness, form dense clumps, and their target vulnerability is negative—meaning they are immune to neighbour competition. These peripheral species have positive vulnerability: whenever a neighbour clumps nearby, their visits are stolen and they receive heavy heterospecific pollen loads, while exerting almost no competitive pressure back.

Symmetrically testing: Who visits? vs What is visited?

Do insects choose the space, or do they only follow the flowers?

Transition from fine-scale space to community-wide synthesis. We saw the effect of time of day (Part 1). We saw that years differ wildly in who is abundant (Part 2). And we just saw that within-meadow spatial neighbourhood strongly modulates visits (Part 2 space slide). The big question: when all these drivers act together, who drives what? For example: does meadow space directly dictate which hoverflies or bees visit a plot (spatial microhabitat, wind, shelter), or does space only matter because different plants grow in different parts of the meadow? Dataset: 15 continuous years (2011–2025) of diurnal surveys at Handrkov, 93 permanent plots mapped with precise coordinates. We slice the data into 3,588 observation units: one plot on one day in one part of the day (morning, midday, afternoon, evening), filtering for at least 5 visits. Method: Redundancy Analysis (RDA) variance partitioning on Hellinger-transformed community matrices. We test 5 sets of predictors: Year (factor), Season (date polynomial), Hour of day (polynomial), Meadow position (cubic trend surface of X and Y), and the composition of the interacting partner (PCA axes). We run this symmetrically in two directions: 1. Explaining the pollinator community (“Who visits?”) 2. Explaining which flowers receive visits (“What is visited?”) By partitioning the variation, we isolate the unique fraction (what that driver explains alone) from the shared fraction (indirect pathways).
